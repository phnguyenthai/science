# Management the Drucker Way

An eight-week, practice-oriented learning program for managers who want to make people and organizations more effective. The program draws on widely known Drucker ideas: management as a practice, contribution and results, customer value, objectives and self-control, strengths, innovation, and responsibility.

## Who this is for

People managers, project leads, founders, and experienced individual contributors preparing to lead. No prior study of Peter Drucker is required.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of the program, participants will be able to:

- Explain an organization's purpose in terms of the people it serves.
- Define their own contribution and connect it to meaningful results.
- Set a small number of clear objectives and review progress against evidence.
- Organize work around people's strengths and improve collaboration.
- Identify opportunities for useful innovation and test them responsibly.
- Make decisions with explicit ownership, follow-through, and learning.

## Format

- **Duration:** 8 weeks
- **Weekly commitment:** 2–3 hours
- **Method:** Read, reflect, apply one idea at work, and discuss the result with a peer.
- **Materials:** Selected chapters or essays from Drucker's work, obtained through a library or other authorized source; the weekly prompts below are self-contained.

Keep a learning journal throughout. Use workplace information only when you are authorized to do so, and avoid recording confidential personal or business details.

## Weekly curriculum

### Week 1 — Management is a practice

**Focus:** Management is judged by what it enables people and the organization to accomplish, not by status or theory alone.

**Practice:** Map the work you influence. For each activity, note who benefits, what result it is meant to produce, and how you can tell whether it helped.

**Reflection:** Which activity creates the least useful value? What evidence supports that judgment?

### Week 2 — Purpose and the customer

**Focus:** Begin with the people served and the value they need; do not define purpose only by internal products, processes, or structures.

**Practice:** Interview two customers, users, or internal stakeholders. Ask what they are trying to accomplish, what gets in their way, and what a better outcome would look like.

**Deliverable:** A one-paragraph purpose statement written from the stakeholder's point of view.

### Week 3 — Contribution and effectiveness

**Focus:** Ask what contribution is needed, what results matter, and how your role can make a distinctive difference.

**Practice:** Write a contribution statement for your role. Identify one result that matters to others and one behavior you will change to improve it.

**Reflection:** What should you stop doing, start doing, or do differently?

### Week 4 — Objectives and self-control

**Focus:** Use a few clear objectives to align effort, make expectations visible, and help people manage their own performance.

**Practice:** Agree on up to three objectives with a relevant colleague or manager. For each, specify the result, evidence of progress, timeframe, and dependencies.

**Deliverable:** A one-page objective sheet and a brief weekly review routine.

### Week 5 — Strengths and working relationships

**Focus:** Build performance by using strengths, including your own, while addressing limitations that obstruct results.

**Practice:** Ask a colleague what they rely on you for and what would make working together more effective. Identify one task to redesign around a person's demonstrated strength.

**Reflection:** What conditions help each person do their best work?

### Week 6 — Decisions and responsibility

**Focus:** Make decisions at the right level, include the people affected, clarify who owns action, and review outcomes.

**Practice:** Use a current, low-risk decision. Record the issue, desired outcome, relevant facts, alternatives, decision owner, people to consult, and follow-up date.

**Deliverable:** A decision note, followed by a short review of what happened.

### Week 7 — Innovation and useful change

**Focus:** Treat innovation as disciplined work: notice change or unmet needs, form a testable idea, and learn from results.

**Practice:** Choose one small opportunity to improve a customer or colleague outcome. State the problem, your hypothesis, a low-cost test, a success signal, and a stop condition.

**Deliverable:** A brief experiment report, including what did not work.

### Week 8 — Integration and operating practice

**Focus:** Connect purpose, contribution, objectives, strengths, decisions, and innovation into repeatable management habits.

**Practice:** Present a case from the program: the situation, action, result, evidence, and next step. Ask a peer to challenge the assumptions.

**Deliverable:** A 90-day management practice plan with three commitments, measures, review dates, and a learning partner.

## Weekly session template

Use this 60-minute agenda for a cohort or peer-learning session:

1. **Check-in (5 min):** What did you try?
2. **Concept (10 min):** Name the week's management idea in your own words.
3. **Case discussion (20 min):** Examine one participant's real, appropriately anonymized example.
4. **Practice review (15 min):** Compare intended outcomes with evidence.
5. **Commitment (10 min):** State the next action, owner, and review date.

## Assessment

This is a practice program, not a test of memorized quotations. Assess progress using:

- The quality and clarity of the participant's purpose and contribution statements.
- Whether objectives describe observable results and receive regular review.
- Examples of work redesigned to use strengths more effectively.
- A documented decision and a completed follow-up.
- An innovation experiment with an explicit hypothesis and evidence.
- A realistic 90-day plan that includes learning and accountability.

Ask participants to assess both outcomes and their own contribution. Avoid treating activity completion as proof of impact.

## Facilitator guidance

- Invite participants to adapt the practices to their roles and organizational context.
- Distinguish Drucker's concepts from the facilitator's interpretation; use authorized primary sources for deeper study.
- Encourage specific evidence, respectful disagreement, and small experiments.
- Do not promise that any single management method guarantees results.
- Protect confidentiality and ensure workplace experiments are ethical, authorized, and appropriately low-risk.
